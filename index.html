Research

Plants are constantly deciding how to respond to other species. A parasitic plant has to recognize a suitable host before it attaches, and a flower has to tell its own pollen apart from the pollen of its neighbors. My work asks how these recognition systems evolve and what they leave behind in plant genomes, combining fieldwork with comparative genomics, transcriptomics, and phylogenomics.

Parasitic plant genomes

Parasitic plants invade their hosts with a novel organ, the haustorium. To ask where the genes behind it came from, I assembled a chromosome-scale reference genome of Pedicularis groenlandica and sequenced transcriptomes across haustorial development, from first contact with a host to mature attachment. Of the 5,635 genes associated with haustoria, two thirds are also linked to pollen tubes and only one third to roots, suggesting that parasites borrowed heavily from the machinery pollen tubes use to grow through another plant’s tissue (Cohen et al. 2026, Annals of Botany).

With Prof. Liming Cai, I also developed HGTScanner, a Python tool that detects horizontal gene transfer in organelle genomes and identifies the donor lineage. Applied to Amborella, it recovered known moss and algal transfers and uncovered DNA from the parasitic plant Hachettea, which shares its range in New Caledonia (Cai and Cohen 2026, Methods in Ecology and Evolution).

As a postdoc in the Cai lab at the University of Florida, I am extending this work to comparative and population genomics of parasitic plants.

Pollen recognition across species

In species-rich meadows, flowers routinely receive pollen from their neighbors. Working at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory, I crossed 11 co-flowering species and followed foreign pollen from the stigma toward the ovules. Heterospecific pollen germinated and grew the full length of the style even between species more than 100 million years diverged, and phylogenetic distance did not predict its success (Cohen et al. 2025, American Journal of Botany).

Pistils do respond differently to foreign pollen, though. Their transcriptional response to pollination fades steadily with evolutionary distance, and heterospecific pollen tubes grow more slowly than a species’ own, slowest of all among close relatives. Rather than actively rejecting foreign pollen, pistils seem to gradually stop supporting it (Cohen et al., preprint).

My dissertation, The Genetics of Recognition: Parasitism, Reproduction, and Genome Evolution in Flowering Plants, received Columbia’s Don Jay Melnick Award in 2026.

About me

I grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area and studied Biology at Amherst College, then did my PhD in the Eaton lab at Columbia University. Fieldwork has taken me to the Colorado Rockies, the Maritime Alps of Italy, Bhutan, Costa Rica, Maryland, Massachusetts, and New York. Before graduate school, I spent a year in Perú’s lowland Amazon as a program assistant for the School for Field Studies, supporting undergraduates in environmental science and biology. I now live in Gainesville, Florida.

Outside the lab I love cooking, playing Scrabble, and hiking.
